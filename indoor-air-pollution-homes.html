The Air Inside Our Homes: An Overlooked Health Concern

By Alpana Pillai

While air pollution is recognized as a major environmental and health concern, its awareness is often limited to outdoor environments, such as roads or industrial areas. However, the air within homes and enclosed spaces can also contain harmful pollutants that pose significant risk to human health. From cooking emissions to ventilation, there are many daily factors that can contribute to a declining IAQ value, and as many individuals spend large portions of their day indoors, the risk it poses is critical. In order to mitigate these risks, and create a safer living environment, it is crucial for individuals to be informed about the sources, risks and preventative measures.

Understanding Indoor Air Pollution

Most awareness regarding air pollution is centered around pollution that originates outdoors, either through vehicle usage, industries or another external factor. This lack of information regarding indoor pollution makes the understanding and exploration of indoor sources of air pollution crucial.

Indoor pollution is classified as the presence of solid, liquid or gaseous pollutants in an enclosed space. These pollutants often include volatile organic compounds (VOCs) for example, and often have a harmful impact on human health, making their reduction extremely critical. The presence of these pollutants determines the IAQ (Indoor Air Quality), which is a value that rates the air quality within a space. Additionally, while outdoor pollution is more commonly talked about, indoor pollution can also reach similar levels of danger due to ventilation. While both indoor and outdoor air pollution contain pollutants, the type of pollutants differs greatly. While both indoor and outdoor air can contain harmful gases and particulate matter, indoor air tends to originate from household activities, furniture and building materials.

Sources of Indoor Air Pollution:

  • Furniture & Decorations - During application, or even months after, a lot of furniture and materials used to build a house tend to release pollutants, most commonly VOCs.
  • Human Activities - The other most common way in which pollutants get released into an indoor atmosphere is through human actions. Activities such as cooking, smoking, usage of household products, like candles or cleaning products for example, can all contribute to the pollution levels indoors.

The sources of indoor air pollution are diverse, and as they are part of everyday life, recognising them is crucial to be aware of what daily habits lead to long-term health complications.

The Impact of Indoor Air Pollution

The effect of indoor air pollution is dependent on the duration of exposure, the type of pollutant and the concentration in the air. While some pollutants lead to immediate irritation, prolonged exposure can lead to extreme health consequences, hence making it crucial to maintain good indoor air quality.

  • Respiratory Health - Fine particulate matter, mold, and smoke can worsen and lead to respiratory conditions such as asthma, bronchitis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Additionally, WHO states that around 22% of pneumonia-related deaths in adults is due to indoor household fuels, showing the pneumonia-related health risk that poor IAQ creates.
  • Cardiovascular Health - Indoor air pollutants can enter the bloodstream and cause systemic inflammation, which can increase the risk of ischaemic heart disease, and other cardiovascular issues such as strokes, high blood pressure and heart rate. A study done on the elderly in Taiwan also shows an association with the negative impact of poor air quality and systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and heart rate.
  • Neurological Health - High levels of exposure to indoor pollutants such as carbon monoxide and other chemical fumes can increase susceptibility to neurological issues such as headaches, dizziness, mental fog, and fatigue. The presence of the pollutants and a poor IAQ can also lead to poor cognitive function.
  • Cancers - Long-term exposure to carcinogens such as radon, second-hand smoke and certain VOCs can all significantly increase the risk of lung cancer.

While all these health effects are serious complications, they become increasingly severe when the individual has prolonged exposure to high concentrations of the pollutants and carcinogens. Hence, the health consequences of indoor air pollution extend far beyond discomfort, it has the potential to affect multiple organs and systems, making prevention extremely crucial.

Improving Indoor Air Quality

Improving air quality involves identifying sources of pollutant and adopting practices that can cleanse and maintain the air within enclosed spaces. Although not every pollutant can be completely eliminated, practical household measures can greatly reduce the amount of exposure, helping create cleaner indoor environments.

One of the main ways in which air quality inside an enclosed space can be improved is through ventilation. The introduction of outdoor air, through opening windows or exhaust fans for example, can help clear out fumes that lead to indoor pollution levels increasing. For instance, an exhaust fan in the kitchen can greatly aid in clearing out fumes from cooking. Another major method of improving air quality is through the installation of air cleaners. Air cleaners, especially HEPA filters, are great solutions for removing existent airborne particulate matter, and hence restoring the air quality to its original state. Apart from the two major solutions of ventilation and filtration, other small steps can also be taken.

  • Smoking outdoors
  • Use cleaner cooking fuels
  • Control mold

Improving indoor air quality requires careful changes from individuals, and certain habitual changes and modifications to the home can be of great aid when striving for a higher IAQ. Although individual actions play an important role, wider access to cleaner energy and healthier housing is equally necessary to ensure that everyone can breathe cleaner air indoors.

Indoor air pollution is an often overlooked health concern, but its impacts are real, ranging from respiratory complications to severe cardiovascular and other long-term issues. Aiding individuals in this health concern does not simply involve change of individual habits, but also incentives regarding awareness and cleaner energy and appliances. Ultimately, improving human health involves looking equally towards the air outdoors and the environments in which we spend most of our time.
